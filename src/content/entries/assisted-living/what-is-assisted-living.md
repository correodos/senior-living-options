Maybe a doctor mentioned it after a fall. Maybe a sibling brought it up on the phone, or you noticed the mail piling up at your mom's house. However the words "assisted living" came up, you probably have the same first question most families have: what is assisted living, exactly, and is it the same as a nursing home?

It isn't. And the difference matters, because choosing the wrong level of care can mean paying for help your loved one doesn't need, or not getting help they do.

This article explains assisted living in plain terms: who actually lives there, what "help with daily activities" means in practice, what a normal day looks like, and what assisted living does not do. ## What Is Assisted Living? A Simple Definition

Assisted living is a home with help built in. Residents live in their own apartment or room, and staff are on hand to help with the personal care tasks that have become hard to manage alone.

The National Institute on Aging describes assisted living as being for people who need help with daily care, but not as much help as a nursing home provides. According to the NIA, communities usually offer:

- Up to three meals a day
- Help with personal care, such as bathing and dressing
- Help with medications
- Housekeeping and laundry
- 24-hour supervision, security and on-site staff
- Social and recreational activities

It helps to picture where assisted living sits among the options. Independent living offers housing and community but no personal care. A [nursing home](/article/nursing-homes/nursing-homes-complete-guide/) provides 24-hour skilled nursing and medical care. Assisted living sits between the two: more support than a regular apartment, far less medical care than a nursing facility. For a side-by-side look at all the options, see our [comparison of the 7 types of senior living](/article/caregiver-resources/senior-living-options-complete-comparison-of-all-7-types/).

## Who Lives in Assisted Living?

It is easier to judge whether assisted living fits your family when you know who is already there. The CDC's National Center for Health Statistics surveys residents of assisted living and similar residential care communities. Its most recent profile, from 2022, shows an older population that needs regular help.

| **Resident characteristic (2022)** | **Share of residents** |
| --- | --- |
| **Women** | 67% |
| **Age 85 and older** | 53% |
| **Age 75 to 84** | 31% |
| **Age 65 to 74** | 10% |
| **Younger than 65** | 6% |
| **Diagnosed with Alzheimer's disease or another dementia** | 44% |
| **Enrolled in Medicaid** | 17% |

*Note: The CDC figures cover "residential care communities," which include assisted living as well as personal care homes, adult care homes, board and care homes and adult foster care. These are the most recent resident figures the CDC has published; results from the 2024 survey had not been published as of October 2026.*

Two numbers stand out. More than half of residents are 85 or older. And about two in five live with dementia (2022). Some assisted living communities have special Alzheimer's units where staff check on and care for residents with dementia. For people who need that level of support, see our guide to [memory care](/article/memory-care/memory-care-complete-guide/).

## What "Help With Daily Activities" Actually Means

You will hear the phrase "activities of daily living," or ADLs, on every tour and in every assessment. It has a specific meaning.

The federal long-term care glossary defines ADLs as the basic actions a person who functions independently does every day, such as bathing, dressing, eating and transferring (moving from a bed to a chair, for example). A second group, called instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs), covers tasks that are not needed for basic functioning but are needed to live on your own, like light housework, preparing meals and taking medication.

In 2022, this is how many residential care residents needed help with each basic activity:

| **Daily activity** | **Residents who needed help (2022)** |
| --- | --- |
| **Bathing** | 75% |
| **Walking** | 71% |
| **Dressing** | 60% |
| **Using the toilet** | 51% |
| **Eating** | 28% |
| **Three or more of these activities** | **62%** |

Bathing is the most common need, and eating the least common. In other words, most residents still eat on their own but need a hand in the shower.

When you visit a community, ask how the staff will assess your loved one's ADLs and IADLs. The assessment usually decides the care plan and, in many communities, the monthly price. The NIA notes that communities often offer a few levels of care, and residents pay more when they need extra services.

## What Daily Life in Assisted Living Looks Like

Day to day, the services listed above shape life in assisted living: meals are provided, help with personal care and medications comes from staff, housekeeping and laundry are handled, and staff, supervision and security are on site 24 hours a day. Communities also offer social and recreational activities. Your loved one still has their own room or apartment.

Beyond that, routines vary a lot from one community to another. The best way to see what a normal day looks like is to see it yourself. On your visits, ask:

- What does a typical day look like for a resident who needs help with bathing and medications?
- What activities are on this month's calendar, and how many residents usually take part?
- How does a resident call for help from their room, and how quickly does staff respond at night?
- Can residents keep their own schedule for waking up, meals and visitors?

> If you can, visit at different times of day, including a mealtime. You will learn more about daily life from lunch in the dining room than from a scheduled tour.

## What Assisted Living Does Not Provide

Knowing the limits of assisted living can prevent a painful second move later.

- **Round-the-clock skilled nursing.** Assisted living is not designed for people who need constant medical care. That is the role of a nursing home.
- **Medicare coverage of the stay.** Medicare does not pay for long-term care, which it also calls custodial care. That includes help with daily activities in an assisted living facility. You pay all costs unless another program helps.
- **The same rules everywhere.** What a community is allowed to do, such as which medications staff may give or which health needs it can accept, depends on state rules and on that community's license.

If your loved one's needs are likely to grow, ask each community how long a resident can stay as care needs increase, and what happens when they need more care than the community is licensed to give.

## Why Assisted Living Has Different Names in Different States

There is no single national rulebook for assisted living. Each state licenses these communities and writes its own requirements for things like scope of care, limits on services, staffing and staff training, according to the National Center for Assisted Living's 2025 State Regulatory Review.

That is why the same kind of place can go by different names. Depending on the state, a community that offers housing plus personal care may be licensed as:

- An assisted living facility, residence or community
- A residential care facility
- A personal care home
- An adult care home
- A board and care home

The CDC uses the same mix of names when it surveys these communities nationwide.

State rules also keep changing. In 2025 alone, **18 states**, including the District of Columbia, updated their assisted living regulations. The most common changes were to training requirements for direct care staff (10 states) and for administrators or directors (9 states). Your state's licensing agency has the current rules for your area.

What this means for you: when you search for options, try your state's term as well as "assisted living." To find licensed communities and your local Area Agency on Aging, you can call the Eldercare Locator, a free public service, at 1-800-677-1116.

## How Assisted Living Is Paid For

Most people pay for assisted living themselves. In 2025, the national median cost was **$6,200 per month**, or **$74,400 per year**, up 5% from 2024, according to the CareScout Cost of Care Survey. Prices vary widely by state; you can check yours on our [senior care costs by state](/costs/) page.

Medicare does not cover the stay. Medicaid can help some people through state programs such as home and community-based services (HCBS) waivers, which can pay for care services. Federal Medicaid rules do not allow those waivers to pay for room and board, so rent and meals stay with the resident. In 2022, only 17% of residents in assisted living and similar communities were enrolled in Medicaid.

For the full picture, including Medicaid waivers, VA benefits and long-term care insurance, see the payment section of our [complete guide to assisted living](/article/assisted-living/assisted-living-complete-guide/).

## Is Assisted Living the Right Fit?

Assisted living is often a good match when most of these are true:

1. Your loved one needs help with some daily activities, such as bathing, dressing or medications.
2. They do not need skilled nursing or medical care around the clock.
3. They would benefit from regular meals, company and someone nearby at night.
4. Staying at home safely would take more help than the family can give.

If constant medical care is needed, a nursing home is usually the better fit. If the main need is a few hours of help a week, [in-home care](/article/in-home-care/in-home-care-complete-guide/) may let them stay at home longer. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are activities of daily living in assisted living?

Activities of daily living (ADLs) are basic self-care tasks such as bathing, dressing, eating and transferring, like moving from a bed to a chair. In 2022, 75% of residents in assisted living and similar communities needed help with bathing, and 62% needed help with three or more ADLs, according to the CDC's National Center for Health Statistics.

### What is the difference between ADLs and IADLs?

ADLs are basic self-care tasks, such as bathing, dressing and eating. Instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs) are not needed for basic functioning but are needed to live independently, such as light housework, preparing meals and taking medication, according to the federal long-term care glossary. Assisted living helps with both.

### Who is a good candidate for assisted living?

A good candidate is someone who needs help with some daily activities but does not need round-the-clock medical care. In 2022, 53% of residents in assisted living and similar communities were 85 or older and 67% were women, according to the CDC. Most needed help with at least one daily activity, most often bathing.

### Can someone with dementia live in assisted living?

Often, yes. In 2022, about 44% of residents in assisted living and similar communities had Alzheimer's disease or another dementia, according to the CDC. Some communities have special Alzheimer's units with staff who check on and care for residents with dementia. People who need a secured setting may be better served by memory care.

### Is assisted living the same in every state?

No. Assisted living is regulated mainly by state law, so rules and names vary. Depending on the state, the same kind of community may be licensed as assisted living, residential care, a personal care home or an adult care home. In 2025 alone, 18 states, including D.C., updated their assisted living rules. Rules on staff training, medications and who can be admitted differ by state.
