## Key Takeaways

- **Assisted living bridges the gap** between independent living and nursing home care — for those who need help with daily activities but not 24/7 medical care.
- **National median cost:** $6,200/month ($74,400/year) as of 2025, up 5% from 2024.
- **Medicare does not cover assisted living** — it only covers specific medical services, not room, board, or personal care.
- **Medicaid may help** through state HCBS waiver programs (46 states + D.C.), but typically covers care services only, not room and board.
- **VA Aid and Attendance** provides up to $2,424/month for qualifying veterans and $1,558/month for surviving spouses (2026 rates).
- **~41,465 communities** with nearly 1.4 million licensed beds exist nationwide.

---

Making the decision to move a loved one into assisted living is one of the hardest things a family can do. You're balancing their safety against their independence, weighing costs you may not have planned for, and doing all of it while managing your own worry and grief. You deserve clear, honest information — not a sales pitch.

This guide covers everything you need to make a confident decision: what assisted living actually is (and isn't), the warning signs that it might be time, what you'll really pay, how Medicaid and VA benefits can help, and exactly what to look for when you visit a facility. The information here comes from federal health agencies and independent research, not from facilities trying to fill beds.

## What Is Assisted Living?

Assisted living is a residential care option for older adults who need help with daily activities but don't require the round-the-clock medical care that a nursing home provides. The goal is to support independence while filling in the gaps — help with bathing, dressing, medications, meals, and housekeeping, combined with 24-hour supervision and a community environment.

Facilities range in size from as few as 25 residents to 100 or more. Most offer private apartments or rooms with shared common areas. A typical community provides:

- Three meals a day in a communal dining room
- Personal care assistance (bathing, dressing, grooming)
- Medication management and reminders
- Laundry and housekeeping services
- 24-hour staff on-site and emergency call systems
- Social activities, fitness programs, and transportation

Some communities also offer specialized memory care units for residents with Alzheimer's disease or other forms of dementia. These units typically have secured entrances and staff trained specifically in dementia care. → [Learn more about memory care](/category/memory-care/)

Assisted living is regulated at the **state level**, not federally, which means requirements and terminology vary significantly. Depending on where you live, these facilities may be called residential care facilities, adult care homes, personal care homes, or board and care homes.

### Assisted Living vs. Nursing Home: Key Differences

These two types of care are often confused, but they serve different needs.

| | Assisted Living | Nursing Home (Skilled Nursing Facility) |
|---|---|---|
| **Care level** | Help with ADLs; some health monitoring | 24/7 skilled nursing and medical care |
| **Who it's for** | Mostly independent adults needing support | People with serious, ongoing medical needs |
| **Living arrangements** | Private apartment or room | Semi-private or private room |
| **Median monthly cost (2025)** | $6,200 | $10,798 (private room) |
| **Medicare coverage** | Not covered | Covered short-term under specific conditions |
| **Medicaid coverage** | Possible via state waiver programs | Covered for those who qualify |

## Signs It May Be Time for Assisted Living

Knowing when to make the move is often the hardest part. There's rarely a single obvious moment — it usually builds over time. According to the National Institute on Aging, these warning signs during visits with a loved one suggest they may need more support:

### At Home
- Meals not being prepared, refrigerator empty, or expired food on shelves
- Medications not being taken correctly or on schedule
- Home is becoming cluttered, dirty, or unsafe
- Bills going unpaid, financial confusion

### Physical Changes
- Difficulty with bathing, dressing, or using the bathroom
- Frequent falls or near-falls
- Significant unexplained weight loss
- Chronic conditions — diabetes, heart disease, COPD — becoming harder to manage alone

### Mental and Emotional Changes
- Increasing memory lapses beyond normal forgetfulness
- Getting lost in familiar places or confusion about time and dates
- Signs of depression: withdrawal, loss of interest in activities, persistent sadness
- Wandering, especially at night (a key safety concern in dementia)

### Caregiver Strain
When family members are providing care, burnout is real. If caregiving is affecting your own health, work, relationships, or finances, that's also a signal that professional help may be needed — for everyone involved.

> The NIA recommends having a direct, honest conversation with your loved one about what you've noticed, framing concerns without criticism and focusing on specific observations: *"I noticed you haven't been eating much. Is shopping getting harder?"*

## What Does Assisted Living Cost?

### National Median Costs (2025)

Assisted living costs have risen significantly over the past several years. According to the **2025 CareScout/Genworth Cost of Care Survey** — the most comprehensive annual study of its kind, based on data from long-term care providers across all 50 states:

| Care Type | Monthly Median | Annual Median |
|---|---|---|
| **Assisted living** | $6,200 | $74,400 |
| **Non-medical caregiver** (home health aide/homemaker) | $6,673 | $80,080 |
| **Nursing home** (private room) | $10,798 | $129,576 |

*Note: Starting with the 2025 survey, CareScout merged the homemaker and home health aide categories into a single "non-medical caregiver" figure, reflecting price convergence between the two service types.*

For context, assisted living costs jumped 10% between 2023 and 2024 (prior median: $5,900/month). The 2025 growth rate has slowed, tracking closer to broader rental housing trends.

### Cost Variation by State

Location is the single biggest factor in how much you'll pay. States with higher costs of living — particularly in the Northeast and West Coast — can run nearly double the national median.

| State | Monthly Median (2025) | Annual Median (2025) |
|---|---|---|
| Mississippi | $4,369 | $52,425 |
| Missouri | $5,400 | $64,800 |
| South Dakota | $4,900 | $58,800 |
| Texas | $5,666 | $67,992 |
| Florida | $5,610 | $67,320 |
| **National median** | **$6,200** | **$74,400** |
| California | $7,000 | $84,000 |
| Maryland | $7,173 | $86,070 |
| New Jersey | $8,710 | $104,520 |
| Massachusetts | $9,600 | $115,200 |
| Hawaii | $12,096 | $145,155 |
| Connecticut | $9,118 | $109,410 |

*CareScout/Genworth Cost of Care Survey 2025 (data collected July–November 2025; published March 2026). [View full 50-state table](https://www.carescout.com/cost-of-care).*

### What's Usually Included — and What Costs Extra

Most facilities charge a **base monthly rate** that covers room, meals, housekeeping, and basic programming. Additional charges apply for higher levels of personal care, memory care, medication management beyond simple reminders, and ancillary services like transportation or physical therapy.

Before signing a contract, ask for a complete fee schedule in writing. Common add-on charges include:

- Personal care tiers (assessed by how many ADLs require assistance)
- Incontinence care and supplies
- Specialty dining for therapeutic diets
- Secure memory care unit (typically $500–$1,500+/month above base rate)
- One-time community or move-in fee

## Who Pays for Assisted Living?

Most families pay out of pocket. This is the reality no one wants to hear, but understanding what you're working with helps you plan.

### Medicare: What It Does and Doesn't Cover

**Medicare does not pay for assisted living.** This is one of the most common misconceptions families encounter. Medicare is health insurance — it covers doctor visits, hospital stays, and certain medical services. It does not cover room and board in assisted living, or the personal care assistance that makes up the bulk of what these communities provide.

Medicare may pay for specific medical services received *while* someone lives in an assisted living facility — such as a visit from a home health aide for wound care, or physical therapy following a hospitalization — but those are separate from the facility's fees.

### Medicaid: Possible, But Complex

Medicaid is the primary public payer for long-term care in the United States, but its role in assisted living is complicated. Unlike nursing home care, which Medicaid covers directly in every state, assisted living coverage is optional and varies dramatically.

As of 2024, 46 states and Washington, D.C. offer some level of Medicaid assistance for people in assisted living or similar residential care settings. Most do this through **Home and Community Based Services (HCBS) waivers**, which allow Medicaid to fund care services in non-nursing-home settings. → [Medicaid.gov HCBS info](https://www.medicaid.gov/medicaid/home-community-based-services/index.html)

**What Medicaid typically covers through waivers:**
- Personal care assistance (help with ADLs)
- Medication management
- Supervision and safety monitoring

**What Medicaid almost never covers:** room and board. Even if you qualify for a waiver, you'll still pay for housing separately.

**Eligibility basics for most state waivers (2024):**
- Income limit: approximately $2,982/month (equal to 300% of the SSI federal benefit rate; varies by state — California caps at $1,801/month, New York at $1,836/month)
- Asset limit: generally $2,000 in countable assets for individuals
- Functional requirement: must demonstrate need for nursing-facility-level care, assessed through a state evaluation

Waitlists are common. If you think your loved one may eventually qualify, applying early matters. Contact your state's Medicaid office or local Area Agency on Aging (Eldercare Locator: 1-800-677-1116) to get state-specific information. → [Eldercare Locator](https://eldercare.acl.gov)

### VA Benefits for Veterans

If your loved one is a veteran — or the surviving spouse of a veteran — the **VA Aid and Attendance benefit** can provide meaningful financial help. This is a monthly pension supplement, not a healthcare benefit, and it can be used toward assisted living costs directly.

**2026 maximum monthly benefit rates** (effective December 1, 2025 through November 30, 2026):

| Status | Monthly Maximum | Annual Maximum |
|---|---|---|
| Single veteran | $2,424 | $29,093 |
| Veteran with one dependent | $2,874 | $34,488 |
| Surviving spouse | $1,558 | $18,697 |

*Rates increased 2.8% from 2025, matching the Social Security COLA.*

To qualify, veterans must have served at least 90 days of active duty including at least one day during a wartime period, and meet both a functional need (requiring help with at least two ADLs) and financial eligibility requirements. The net worth limit for 2026 is **$163,699** (excluding the primary home and vehicle).

Contact your local VA regional office or a VA-accredited claims agent to start the application process. Forms: VA Form 21P-527EZ (veterans) and VA Form 21P-534EZ (surviving spouses).

### Long-Term Care Insurance

If your loved one purchased a long-term care insurance policy, review it carefully. Many policies cover assisted living, but benefit triggers, elimination periods (the waiting period before benefits kick in), and daily benefit maximums vary widely. Pull out the policy document and call the insurer directly to confirm what's covered and how to file a claim. → [LongTermCare.gov](https://longtermcare.acl.gov/)

## What Services Does Assisted Living Provide?

Across the country, the core services offered in assisted living are fairly consistent. Where communities differ is in the *level* of care they can provide, their specialty programs, and the quality of their environment and staff.

### Standard Services (Included in Base Rate)
- 24-hour awake staff and emergency call systems in each room
- Meals (typically three per day, plus snacks)
- Assistance with bathing, dressing, grooming, and toileting
- Medication administration or management
- Housekeeping and laundry
- Scheduled activities and social programming
- Transportation to medical appointments

### Services That Vary by Facility
- On-site licensed nursing staff
- Physical, occupational, or speech therapy
- Specialized dementia or memory care programs
- Hospice care coordination
- Religious or cultural programming
- Outdoor spaces, fitness equipment, or pool access

According to NCAL, about 80% of assisted living residents and their families report being satisfied with their care. That said, satisfaction varies significantly by facility, so site visits and staff conversations remain essential.

## How to Choose an Assisted Living Facility

### Step 1: Define the Care Needs
Start by making a list of what your loved one needs today — and what they're likely to need in the next two to three years. The NIA recommends asking a healthcare provider to help assess functional needs, particularly if cognitive decline or chronic conditions are involved.

**Key questions to clarify:**
- How many activities of daily living (ADLs) require help?
- Is there a memory condition that needs specialized care?
- Are there specific medical needs (insulin administration, wound care, oxygen)?
- What level of physical mobility and social engagement does the person have?

Some facilities can only accommodate lower-care residents. If your loved one's needs will likely increase, choosing a facility that offers multiple levels of care — or is part of a Continuing Care Retirement Community (CCRC) — can prevent a disruptive second move later.

### Step 2: Narrow Your List
Use the **Eldercare Locator** (eldercare.acl.gov or 1-800-677-1116) to find licensed facilities in your area. Your local Area Agency on Aging can also provide referrals and information about complaint histories. The NIA also recommends asking physicians, social workers, and trusted friends for recommendations.

### Step 3: Visit in Person — More Than Once
A website and brochure tell you nothing about what daily life actually looks like. Before choosing any facility, visit in person. The NIA specifically recommends making at least one **unannounced visit** — a different day of the week, at a different time of day — to see how the facility operates when it's not expecting you.

**During your visits, pay attention to:**

| Area | What to Look For |
|---|---|
| **Staff interactions** | Are staff engaging warmly with residents, or rushing past them? Do they know residents by name? |
| **Residents' demeanor** | Do they appear alert and engaged, or sedated and isolated? |
| **Physical environment** | Is it clean and free of odors? Are common areas inviting? |
| **Dining** | Stop by at a mealtime. Is the food appealing? Do residents have choices? |
| **Safety features** | Grab bars, non-slip flooring, secured exits. Dementia-safe if needed? |
| **Activities** | Is a calendar posted? Are residents actually participating, or is a TV on in an empty room? |

AARP advises that you also pay attention to how you *feel* walking through the space — whether the atmosphere feels like a home or an institution.

### Step 4: Ask the Hard Questions
Don't let marketing language substitute for direct answers. The NIA recommends meeting with the director of nursing and key care staff, not just the admissions coordinator.

**Questions worth asking:**
- What is the staff-to-resident ratio during day and night shifts?
- What is the staff turnover rate? (High turnover is a quality warning sign.)
- How are care plans developed, and how often are they updated?
- What happens if a resident's needs exceed what the facility can provide?
- What are the discharge and transfer policies?
- How are families notified of health changes or incidents?
- What is your policy on arbitration clauses in contracts?

### Step 5: Review the Contract Carefully
Before signing anything, read the entire contract — and have a lawyer review it if possible. AARP specifically advises being alert to **arbitration clauses**, which can limit your legal options if something goes wrong.

Verify that everything promised verbally is in writing, including:
- Specific services included in the base rate
- Process for care plan modifications
- Fee increase policies
- What happens to a resident who runs out of funds

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does Medicare pay for assisted living?
No. Medicare does not cover assisted living. Medicare is health insurance and does not pay for housing, personal care assistance, or daily support services provided in assisted living facilities. It may pay for specific medical services (like physical therapy or home health aide visits) that a resident receives while living there, but those are billed separately from facility fees.

### How much does assisted living cost per month in 2025?
The national median is **$6,200 per month ($74,400 per year)** according to the 2025 CareScout/Genworth Cost of Care Survey — a 5% increase from 2024. Costs vary significantly by state and city, ranging from roughly $3,800–$4,400/month in lower-cost states to $8,500+/month in states like Massachusetts and New Jersey.

### What is the difference between assisted living and a nursing home?
Assisted living is for people who need help with daily activities but can live semi-independently. Nursing homes (skilled nursing facilities) provide 24/7 medical and nursing care for people with serious, ongoing health needs. Nursing homes are more medically intensive and significantly more expensive — the national median for a private room in 2024 was $10,646/month.

### Will Medicaid pay for assisted living?
Possibly, depending on your state and your loved one's income and care needs. As of 2024, 46 states and D.C. offer some form of Medicaid assistance for assisted living through Home and Community Based Services (HCBS) waivers. However, Medicaid typically covers care services only — not room and board — and there are often waitlists. Income and asset limits apply. Contact your state Medicaid office or call the Eldercare Locator at 1-800-677-1116 for state-specific information.

### What questions should I ask when visiting an assisted living facility?
The most important questions cover staffing (ratios and turnover), care plan development and frequency, what happens if needs increase, discharge policies, and how families are kept informed. The NIA recommends also making an unscheduled visit to see the facility's day-to-day reality, not just what's shown during a planned tour.

### Are there financial assistance programs for veterans in assisted living?
Yes. Wartime veterans and their surviving spouses may qualify for the **VA Aid and Attendance** pension benefit, which in 2026 provides up to $2,424/month for a single veteran and up to $1,558/month for a surviving spouse (rates effective December 1, 2025). The benefit can be used directly toward assisted living costs. Contact your local VA regional office or a VA-accredited claims agent to apply.
