> - At those medians, the two cost about the same at roughly **41 hours of home care a week** (our calculation). Below that, home care usually costs less. Above it, assisted living usually costs less.

You may be sitting with a hard question: should your mother or father stay at home with paid help, or move to a community with care built in? Neither answer is wrong. Both can work well. The right one depends on how much help is needed, how much of the day your parent is alone, and what your family can pay.

This article compares assisted living vs in-home care in four areas: cost, safety, daily life and who pays. All cost figures come from the 2025 CareScout Cost of Care Survey, the most recent edition published. A median means half of the prices were higher and half were lower, so your local prices will differ. Use these figures as a yardstick and ask for written quotes.

If you want the full picture of either option first, read our [complete guide to assisted living](/article/assisted-living/assisted-living-complete-guide/) or our [complete guide to in-home care](/article/in-home-care/in-home-care-complete-guide/).

## Assisted Living vs In-Home Care: The Short Answer

Assisted living gives your parent a place to live, meals and help with daily tasks, with staff always on site. In-home care brings a caregiver to your parent's own home for the hours you pay for. This table puts the main differences side by side.

| Feature | Assisted living | In-home care |
| ------- | --------------- | ------------ |
| **Where** | A community apartment or room | Your parent's own home |
| **Staff on site** | 24 hours a day | Only during paid hours |
| **You pay for** | A monthly fee | Each hour of care |
| **Median cost (2025)** | **$6,200 per month** | **$35 per hour** |
| **Medicare pays** | No | Only part-time skilled care |

*Note: "In-home care" here means a non-medical caregiver who helps with bathing, dressing and household tasks. Skilled nursing at home is priced and paid for differently. Our [in-home care cost guide](/article/in-home-care/how-much-does-in-home-care-cost-per-month/) explains both.*

## Assisted Living vs In-Home Care Cost

The two prices are built differently, so they cannot be compared directly. Assisted living is a monthly fee. In-home care is an hourly rate, and the monthly bill depends on how many hours you buy.

### The Hours That Make the Two Cost the Same

The 2025 survey reports a national median of **$6,200 per month** for a one-bedroom assisted living unit and **$35 per hour** for a non-medical home caregiver. CareScout's monthly home care figure assumes **44 hours a week**, which comes to **$6,673 per month**.

Here is what the hourly rate costs at different weekly hours. These are our calculations from the $35 median:

| Home care hours per week | Monthly cost | Compared with $6,200 |
| ------------------------ | ------------ | -------------------- |
| **10 hours** | $1,517 | Lower |
| **20 hours** | $3,033 | Lower |
| **30 hours** | $4,550 | Lower |
| **41 hours** | $6,218 | About the same |
| **56 hours** (8 hours a day) | $8,493 | Higher |
| **168 hours** (24 hours a day) | $25,480 | About four times higher |

The 168-hour row is a plain multiplication, not a price anyone quotes. CareScout does not publish a national median for live-in or 24-hour care, so ask local agencies how they price nights and overnight coverage.

### Costs Differ by State

The break-even point moves with local prices. These are our calculations from each state's 2025 medians:

| State | Assisted living | Home care hourly | Hours a week to match |
| ----- | --------------- | ---------------- | --------------------- |
| **Wyoming** | $5,325 | $46 | 27 |
| **Illinois** | $6,219 | $36 | 40 |
| **California** | $7,000 | $40 | 40 |
| **Ohio** | $6,102 | $34 | 41 |
| **Texas** | $5,666 | $30 | 44 |
| **New York** | $7,110 | $35 | 47 |

In Wyoming, home care is expensive and assisted living is not, so the two match at only 27 hours a week. In New York, you can buy about 47 hours of home care before you reach the assisted living median. Find your own state in our [costs by state guide](/costs/).

### What Each Price Leaves Out

The fees do not cover the same things. Ask each provider for a full written list.

- **Assisted living:** The base fee usually covers a room, meals, WiFi, 24-hour staff and activities. Extra costs can include a one-time community fee, yearly rent increases and higher charges as care needs grow.
- **In-home care:** The hourly rate pays the caregiver. Your parent's rent or mortgage, utilities, food and home upkeep are still there. CareScout also lists mileage, home changes such as ramps and grab bars, and overtime charges in some areas for more than 40 hours a week.

That is why a 41-hour week is a rough guide, not a rule. For a closer look at facility prices, read [what assisted living costs per month](/article/assisted-living/how-much-does-assisted-living-cost-per-month/).

## Is In-Home Care or Assisted Living Safer?

Safety is the worry behind most of these decisions, so it deserves a straight answer. Neither setting is automatically safer. What differs is who is nearby when something goes wrong.

Falls are the leading cause of injury for adults 65 and older. About **1 in 4** older adults, more than 14 million people, report falling each year. In 2024, falls caused over **43,000 deaths** among adults 65 and older. They also led to about **4.5 million** emergency department visits.

The CDC pages we reviewed do not compare fall rates at home with fall rates in assisted living. We will not claim that one setting has fewer falls. The question that matters is what happens next.

### Staff Nearby vs Hours Between Visits

In assisted living, staff are on site 24 hours a day. CareScout says this can bring more peace of mind if no one lives nearby. At home, a caregiver is there only for the hours you pay for. In the hours between visits, your parent is on their own unless someone else is there.

CareScout suggests that families who choose home care build a "care circle" of family, friends or neighbors who can step in just in case. If your parent lives alone and nobody is close, that gap is the main risk to weigh.

### Making a Home Safer

Many older adults plan to change their home. In AARP's 2024 survey, **43%** of older adults wanted changes so their home could handle future physical limits. Among those planning changes, **72%** would upgrade the bathroom, for example with grab bars and non-slip tile. Another **71%** would make it easier to enter or move around the home, with ramps or chairlifts.

These changes cost money, and they only help if the home can be changed. Stairs, a narrow bathroom or a distant bedroom can limit what is possible.

### When Needs Are Higher

Assisted living is not only for people who are nearly independent. In 2022, **75%** of residents in residential care communities needed help with bathing and **60%** needed help with dressing. About **44%** had Alzheimer disease or another dementia. If your parent cannot be left alone for long stretches, hours of paid home care add up fast, as the table above shows.

Assisted living has limits too. If your parent needs ongoing skilled nursing, ask about the community's discharge rules. Our article [Assisted Living vs Nursing Home](/article/assisted-living/assisted-living-vs-nursing-home/) explains where those limits fall.

## Daily Life, Independence and Loneliness

Money and safety are only part of it. Your parent's wishes count too. In AARP's 2024 survey, **75%** of adults 50 and older wanted to stay in their current home as long as possible. Home care is often how that wish becomes possible.

Staying put also keeps familiar rooms, neighbors and routines. Moving gives up some of that. In exchange, assisted living offers meals, housekeeping, help with daily tasks and on-site activities.

Loneliness matters for health. A National Academies report found strong evidence that social isolation and loneliness in older adults are associated with early death, dementia and heart disease. Association is not proof of cause. Still, CareScout says assisted living offers many chances to socialize, and it can help people who live alone or far from family.

A paid caregiver can be good company, but visits end. If your parent lives alone, ask how many hours a week they talk with someone other than a paid caregiver. If the answer is few, a day program may help; see [Home Care vs Adult Day Care](/article/in-home-care/home-care-vs-adult-day-care/).

## Who Pays: Medicare and Medicaid

Neither program pays for most of the care families need.

### Medicare

Medicare says it does not pay for long-term care, which it describes as mostly non-medical custodial care. That includes help in assisted living. Medicare does pay for part-time or intermittent skilled care at home, such as nursing or therapy, if your parent is homebound and a doctor orders it. It does not cover 24-hour care at home, or personal care when that is the only care needed. For details on facilities, read [Does Medicare Cover Assisted Living?](/article/assisted-living/does-medicare-cover-assisted-living/)

### Medicaid

Medicaid may pay for care in the community through Home and Community-Based Services (HCBS) waivers. Services can include homemaker help, home health aide, personal care, adult day health and respite care. Each state decides what it offers and how many people it serves.

Federal rules do not allow waiver money to pay for room and board, with narrow exceptions. In assisted living, that matters because the monthly fee includes the room and meals.

Waiting lists are common. About **650,000** people were on HCBS waiver waiting or interest lists in 2026, and about a quarter of them were older adults or adults with physical disabilities. The average wait across all waiver groups was **34 months**. Lists are not directly comparable from state to state, and people on them may qualify for other home care while they wait. Apply early. Our article [What If You Can't Afford Assisted Living?](/article/assisted-living/cant-afford-assisted-living/) covers other ways to pay, and [Is Long-Term Care Insurance Worth It?](/article/senior-care-costs/long-term-care-insurance-worth-it/) explains how a policy can help.

## How to Choose Between Assisted Living and In-Home Care

Start with what your parent needs, not with price. Then check the cost. These signs can help you sort the two options. They are guides, not a diagnosis. Your parent's doctor can describe the care needed today and what is likely to change.

**In-home care may fit when your parent:**

- Needs help for a few hours a day, well under about 41 hours a week.
- Lives in a home that is safe, or can be made safe, with changes such as grab bars or ramps.
- Has family, friends or neighbors close enough to cover the gaps.
- Strongly wants to stay in their own home.

**Assisted living may fit when your parent:**

- Cannot be left alone for long stretches, day or night.
- Needs help at several points in the day, so the paid hours would pass about 41 a week.
- Lives alone with no one nearby, or is isolated.
- Lives in a home that would be hard or costly to make safe.

You do not have to decide forever. Many families start with a few hours of home care and look again when needs change. Others try assisted living with a clear picture of what would make them leave.

### Questions to Answer Before You Decide

1. Which tasks does your parent need help with, and at what times of day?
2. How many hours a day is your parent alone, and who could reach them quickly?
3. What is the total monthly cost of each option, in writing, including extras?
4. What happens when needs rise? Ask an agency about adding hours, and a community about its discharge rules.
5. What does your parent want, and have you asked them directly?

## Frequently Asked Questions

### At what point does assisted living cost less than home care?

At the 2025 national medians, assisted living costs less once you need more than about **41 hours of home care a week** (our calculation). By state, the point ranges from about 27 hours in Wyoming to 47 hours in New York. Your own break-even depends on local rates, overtime charges and what you spend to run the home.

### Is it safer for a parent to live at home or in assisted living?

Neither is automatically safer. Falls are the leading cause of injury for adults 65 and older, and about **1 in 4** older adults fall each year (CDC). Assisted living has staff on site 24 hours a day. At home, safety depends on the hours of care you buy, home changes such as grab bars, and who can reach your parent between visits.

### Does Medicare pay for assisted living or in-home care?

Medicare does not pay for long-term care in either setting. It does pay for part-time skilled care at home, such as nursing or therapy, if your parent is homebound and a doctor orders it. It does not cover 24-hour care at home or personal care alone, and it does not pay the assisted living fee.

### Does Medicaid pay for assisted living or home care?

It can help pay for care at home through state waiver programs. Federal rules do not let waiver money pay for room and board, which matters in assisted living because the fee includes housing and meals. Waivers also have waiting lists: about **650,000** people were on them in 2026, with an average wait of **34 months** across all waiver groups (KFF).

### When should you move from home care to assisted living?

Think about a move when your parent can no longer be safe alone for the hours between visits, when the paid hours pass about **41 a week**, or when the home cannot be made safe. Talk with your parent's doctor first. Ask the community what care it provides and when it would ask a resident to leave.

### What if my parent wants to stay at home?

You are in good company. In 2024, **75%** of adults 50 and older said they want to stay in their current home as long as possible (AARP). Begin with the help needed today, such as a few hours of home care. Set a date to review the plan, so you can add hours or look at assisted living if needs grow.
