> **Key Takeaways**
> - Someone turning 65 today has almost a **70% chance of needing long-term care** at some point — yet most families have no plan for how to pay for it.
> - **Medicare does not pay for long-term care.** It covers up to 100 days of skilled nursing after a hospital stay — nothing more. Assisted living, personal home care, and custodial nursing home care are not covered.
> - **Medicaid is the primary payer** for long-term nursing home care in the U.S., but qualifying requires spending most assets down to roughly $2,000 first (varies by state).
> - The national median cost of senior care ranges from **$24,700/year for adult day health care** to **$129,575/year for a private nursing home room** (2025).
> - There are more payment options than most families realize — including VA benefits, long-term care insurance, reverse mortgages, and state programs. This guide explains all of them.

---

The cost of senior care is one of the largest financial shocks a family can face — and it almost always arrives before anyone is ready. One hospitalization, one fall, one diagnosis, and suddenly you're looking at costs that can easily exceed $8,000 a month, with no clear roadmap for how to cover them.

The hardest part isn't the money itself. It's that the rules are genuinely complicated: what Medicare covers, what it doesn't, when Medicaid kicks in, and how to protect a spouse's assets while qualifying for help. Families make expensive mistakes simply because no one explained the system clearly.

This guide covers every realistic way to pay for senior care — from government programs to private options — with accurate, current figures and no financial products to sell. Whether you're planning ahead or making decisions right now under pressure, you'll find a clear picture of what's available and how each option actually works.

## What Senior Care Actually Costs in 2025

Before looking at how to pay, you need to know what you're paying for. The **2025 Cost of Care Survey by CareScout (Genworth)** — which collected more than 25,000 rates from long-term care providers nationwide between July and November 2025 — provides the most current national data:

| Care Type | Annual Median Cost | Monthly Median |
|---|---|---|
| Adult day health care (5 days/week) | $24,700 | $2,058 |
| Non-medical in-home caregiver (44 hrs/week, $35/hour) | $80,080 | $6,673 |
| Assisted living (private, 1 bedroom) | $74,400 | $6,200 |
| Nursing home — semi-private room | $114,975 | $9,581 |
| Nursing home — private room | $129,575 | $10,798 |

CareScout now reports home health aides and homemakers as one "non-medical caregiver" category, and the 2025 survey added skilled nursing in the home (a private duty nurse) at a national median of $90 per hour or $160 per visit.

These are national medians. Costs in high-cost states can be 50% or more above the median — a semi-private nursing home room has a state median of $15,208 per month in Connecticut and $27,831 in Alaska. Lower-cost states can be 30–40% below the median, such as Texas ($5,627 per month) for a semi-private nursing home room. Browse our [senior care costs by state](/costs/) for all 50 states, or use the interactive tool at **carescout.com/cost-of-care** to look up figures for your specific location.

The critical planning reality: someone who needs nursing home care for two years will spend nearly $230,000 at the median rate for a semi-private room. That number sits well beyond most families' savings — which is exactly why knowing your payment options matters before a crisis forces the decision.

## Medicare Senior Care Coverage: What It Pays (and What It Doesn't)

Most people assume Medicare will cover their long-term care needs. It won't — and understanding exactly where Medicare stops is the foundation of any payment plan.

**What Medicare does cover** for senior care:

**Skilled nursing facility care (short-term only).** Medicare Part A pays for up to 100 days in a skilled nursing facility per benefit period — but only after a qualifying hospital inpatient stay of at least 3 days, and only as long as skilled care is medically necessary. For 2026, the cost structure is:

- **Days 1–20:** $0 — Medicare pays 100%
- **Days 21–100:** $217/day coinsurance — you pay, Medicare pays the rest
- **Day 101+:** You pay everything — Medicare pays nothing

**Skilled home health care.** Medicare covers nursing visits, physical/occupational/speech therapy, and home health aide services at home — when a doctor certifies the need, the patient is homebound, and a Medicare-certified agency provides the care. When these conditions are met, the patient pays nothing for covered visits.

**What Medicare does not cover** — regardless of how much care is needed:

- Long-term nursing home care once the 100-day window expires
- Assisted living (at all, ever)
- Personal home care (bathing, dressing, meal prep) when that's the only need
- 24-hour custodial care at home
- Memory care in any setting

The fundamental rule: Medicare pays for acute and short-term skilled care. It does not pay for the ongoing daily support that most seniors eventually need.

**Medicare Advantage (Part C)** plans sometimes offer supplemental home care benefits beyond original Medicare — but coverage varies widely by plan, and benefits can change each year. Always verify what your specific plan covers.

## Medicaid: The Primary Payer for Long-Term Care

Medicaid covers more long-term care in the United States than any other program — it is the de facto long-term care insurer for Americans who exhaust their own resources. But qualifying for it requires a genuine financial reckoning.

### Who Medicaid Covers

Medicaid is a joint federal-state program with eligibility rules that vary by state. For long-term care coverage, most states require:

- **Income below the state's limit** — for nursing home Medicaid, most states cap monthly income around the cost of nursing home care itself (roughly $2,800–$3,000/month in many states, though this varies). Seniors above this limit may be able to qualify through income spend-down programs in states that allow them.
- **Countable assets below approximately $2,000** — this is the most significant threshold for most families. Nearly all savings, investment accounts, and most other financial assets must be spent down to this level before Medicaid begins paying. The family home, one vehicle, and certain personal property are typically exempt.
- **A nursing home level of care need** — meaning the person's physical or cognitive condition requires skilled or custodial care that couldn't be safely managed in a less intensive setting.

### The Spend-Down Process

Spending down means paying for care out of pocket until assets reach the Medicaid threshold. This is legal, expected, and the way the system is designed — not a loophole to exploit. Countable assets can be spent on:

- Nursing home or assisted living bills
- Medical expenses not covered by insurance
- Home modifications and accessibility equipment
- Prepaid funeral and burial arrangements
- Personal needs and household items

What you cannot do: give assets away to family members to qualify faster. Medicaid has a **5-year look-back period** — any assets transferred to others within 5 years of applying may result in a period of ineligibility, calculated based on the value transferred. This is a firm federal rule.

### Spousal Protections

Federal law protects the "community spouse" — the partner who remains at home when their husband or wife enters a nursing home. The **Community Spouse Resource Allowance (CSRA)** lets the at-home spouse keep between $32,532 and $162,660 of the couple's combined assets (2026 federal range). They also keep their own income. States set the exact amounts within these federal limits.

### Medicaid for Home Care

Medicaid's **Home and Community-Based Services (HCBS) waiver programs** pay for personal care, homemaker services, and home health aide services delivered at home for qualifying low-income seniors. Nearly all states offer at least one HCBS waiver program, but enrollment is capped and waiting lists can be long. Apply early — contact your local Area Agency on Aging at **eldercare.acl.gov** for state-specific information.

## Long-Term Care Insurance: What It Is and How It Works

Long-term care (LTC) insurance is private insurance purchased specifically to cover the costs of [nursing home care](/article/nursing-homes/nursing-homes-complete-guide/), [assisted living](/article/assisted-living/assisted-living-complete-guide/), [home care](/article/in-home-care/in-home-care-complete-guide/), and adult day services. It's most effective when purchased before health problems develop — typically in your 50s or early 60s, when premiums are lower and acceptance is easier.

### How a Policy Works

When you need care, you must first meet two criteria before the policy pays:

**Benefit trigger:** Most tax-qualified LTC policies pay when you need help with **2 of 6 activities of daily living** (bathing, dressing, toileting, eating, transferring, and continence) **or** when you have a **severe cognitive impairment** such as Alzheimer's disease. The insurance company will assess your condition — ideally, your own physician certifies the need.

**Elimination period:** After the benefit trigger is met, you must pay out of pocket for a waiting period before benefits begin. Most policies offer options of 30, 60, or 90 days. Think of this like a deductible measured in time rather than money. The longer you choose, the lower your premiums — but the more you cover yourself before coverage kicks in.

Once both criteria are met, the policy pays a daily or monthly benefit toward your care costs, up to the policy's limits.

### What to Look for in a Policy

Not all LTC policies are equivalent. Before a policy is useful, check:

- **Benefit amount:** The daily or monthly maximum the policy pays. Make sure it's enough to cover meaningful care in your area.
- **Benefit period:** How long benefits will be paid — 2 years, 5 years, or lifetime. Most claims are shorter than people expect, but severe dementia cases can last many years.
- **Inflation protection:** Whether the benefit amount grows over time to keep pace with rising care costs. Without it, a policy bought today may cover only a fraction of care costs in 15–20 years.
- **Covered care settings:** Most current policies cover nursing homes, assisted living, and home care. Confirm all three are included.
- **Waiver of premium:** Many policies stop charging premiums once you're receiving benefits — a significant financial relief.

### Hybrid Policies

Many insurers now sell **hybrid life insurance / LTC policies** that combine death benefit coverage with long-term care benefits. If you never need LTC, your heirs receive the death benefit. These have become popular partly because the standalone LTC market has contracted significantly — covered lives in standalone plans have been declining by roughly 1–3% per year for the past decade, as of 2024. Only about **5.8 million Americans** hold standalone LTC insurance policies today — approximately 7% of people over 60. The top five insurers by in-force premium (Genworth, John Hancock, Northwestern Mutual, MetLife, and Unum) account for nearly 60% of all policyholders. Annual industry claims reached **$17 billion in 2024**, up over 80% since 2015, reflecting an aging policyholder base.

**What standalone LTC premiums look like in 2026.** The American Association for Long-Term Care Insurance (AALTCI) publishes annual benchmarks based on a $165,000 benefit pool with 3% annual inflation protection. For a single healthy applicant purchasing in 2026:

- **Age 55 male:** approximately $950–$1,000/year
- **Age 55 female:** approximately $1,500–$1,600/year (women pay more due to longer lifespans and higher claim rates)
- **Age 60 male:** approximately $1,200/year
- **Age 60 female:** approximately $4,450/year for comparable protection
- **Couple, both age 60:** quotes ranging from roughly $4,591 to $7,173/year depending on insurer

Note that premiums for standalone policies are **not guaranteed to remain fixed** — many insurers have implemented substantial rate increases on in-force policies over the past two decades. This is a real risk to account for when purchasing. The longer the elimination period you choose, the lower the initial premium.

## Veterans Benefits (VA Aid & Attendance)

Wartime veterans and their surviving spouses may be eligible for the **VA Aid & Attendance** benefit — one of the most underutilized programs in the senior care financing landscape.

Aid & Attendance is an enhanced monthly pension paid on top of the basic VA pension, tax-free, to qualifying veterans who need help with daily activities. In 2026 (rates effective December 1, 2025 through November 30, 2026), the maximum monthly amounts are:

| Who Is Applying | Maximum Monthly Benefit |
|---|---|
| Single veteran | $2,424/month |
| Married veteran | $2,874/month |
| Surviving spouse of veteran | $1,558/month |

To qualify, a veteran must: have served at least 90 days of active duty with at least one day during a wartime period; have an other-than-dishonorable discharge; meet care criteria (need help with daily activities, be in a nursing home due to incapacity, or be nearly blind); and have income and net worth below VA limits. The 2026 net worth limit is $163,699.

**Important nuance:** If a veteran is also receiving Medicaid for nursing home care, VA Aid & Attendance benefits are generally reduced to a small personal-needs amount — the VA does not pay for costs that another federal program already covers. For private-pay residents, however, Aid & Attendance can provide meaningful monthly support toward care costs.

Veterans apply by filing VA Form 21P-527EZ; surviving spouses file VA Form 21P-534EZ. Apply directly through VA.gov or contact a VA-accredited claims agent. The VA also operates its own **Community Living Centers** (nursing homes) in many areas.

## Out-of-Pocket and Private Pay

Many families begin paying for senior care entirely on their own — from savings, retirement accounts, or investment income. This is called "private pay," and it gives families the most flexibility in choosing a facility or provider, since not every nursing home or assisted living community accepts Medicaid.

Private pay is common at the start of a care journey, and many families transition to Medicaid after assets are spent down. If that's your family's likely path, plan for it deliberately:

- Identify which facilities in your area accept Medicaid, and choose one from the start — some facilities discharge residents when they switch from private pay to Medicaid, while others guarantee a Medicaid bed.
- Keep documentation of all care expenses; these may count toward a Medicaid spend-down.
- Consult an elder law attorney before making large asset moves, gifts, or transfers.

There is no fixed timeline for how long private savings will last — it depends entirely on the cost of care in your area, how many hours of care are needed, and your starting assets. At the 2025 national median of $9,581/month for a semi-private nursing home room, $100,000 in savings lasts about ten months.

## Reverse Mortgages (HECM)

Homeowning seniors 62 and older may be able to tap home equity through a **Home Equity Conversion Mortgage (HECM)** — the FHA-insured reverse mortgage program administered by the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD).

With a HECM, you borrow against your home's equity and receive the funds as a lump sum, monthly payments, or a line of credit. You continue living in the home and pay no monthly mortgage payments — the loan is repaid when the home is sold, you move out permanently, or you die.

**How it can help pay for care:**
- Monthly HECM payments can supplement income to cover home care costs
- A lump sum can fund a period of assisted living or nursing home care
- A HECM line of credit can be drawn as needed to pay for care on an ongoing basis

**Key conditions and risks:**
- You must remain in the home as your primary residence. If you move to a nursing home permanently, the loan becomes due.
- Property taxes, homeowners insurance, and maintenance must be kept current — failure to do so can trigger default.
- HECMs have fees, including an FHA insurance premium (2% upfront, 0.5% annually) and closing costs. These are typically financed into the loan but reduce the equity available to heirs.
- Federal law requires counseling with an independent HUD-approved counselor before taking out a HECM — this is a meaningful safeguard worth taking seriously.

A HECM works best for seniors who want to stay in their homes and need supplemental income for in-home care, or for those in early stages of a care journey who have substantial home equity. It is not suited for someone who has already moved or will soon move permanently to a facility.

## Other Payment Options to Know

**State-funded programs.** Beyond Medicaid, many states operate their own senior assistance programs — supplemental in-home care subsidies, caregiver support programs, home modification grants, and more. Availability and eligibility vary enormously. Your local **Area Agency on Aging** is the best starting point for identifying what exists in your state. Find yours at **eldercare.acl.gov**.

**Life insurance policy options.** Existing life insurance policies may be usable for care costs through two mechanisms. A **life settlement** involves selling the policy to a third party for more than its cash surrender value but less than the death benefit — the buyer then collects the death benefit when you die. An **accelerated death benefit rider**, if included in your policy, lets you draw on the death benefit while still alive if you have a qualifying condition. Check your policy documents or ask your insurer what options exist.

**Annuities.** Some families use deferred annuities to create a guaranteed income stream for care costs. Certain hybrid annuity-LTC products can be structured to pay enhanced benefits when care is needed. Tax rules for withdrawing from annuities to pay for care are favorable in some cases. Consult a fee-only financial advisor — not someone who earns commissions on product sales — before restructuring annuities.

**Caregiver agreements.** An adult child or other family member providing significant daily care may enter into a formal paid caregiver agreement — a legal contract setting pay rates for services rendered. This is a legitimate way to compensate a family caregiver and can also count toward Medicaid spend-down. It must be properly structured by an elder law attorney to be recognized.

## How to Choose the Right Payment Strategy

Most families end up using a combination of these options, not just one. A common sequence:

1. **Short-term care** (recovery after hospitalization): Medicare covers skilled nursing and home health care for a limited period. No planning needed — but know the 100-day limit.
2. **Moderate ongoing needs** (part-time home care, assisted living): Private pay, supplemented by any LTC insurance, VA benefits, or state programs.
3. **Intensive long-term care** (nursing home, severe dementia): Private pay transitioning to Medicaid spend-down, with possible HECM for homeowners.

The single most useful step you can take if your loved one is not yet in a crisis situation: consult a **certified elder law attorney** (look for an NAELA-member attorney or a CELA — Certified Elder Law Attorney). They can assess your specific financial picture, identify Medicaid eligibility strategies, advise on asset protection for a community spouse, and help structure a plan that preserves what can legally be preserved. Many families spend thousands more than necessary simply because they never got proper advice.

Find your local Area Agency on Aging for referrals to elder law attorneys and financial counselors at **eldercare.acl.gov** or by calling **1-800-677-1116**.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does Medicare pay for long-term senior care?
No. Medicare covers up to 100 days of skilled nursing care after a qualifying hospital stay of at least 3 days, and it covers skilled home health care when a doctor orders it. It does not cover assisted living, custodial nursing home care, personal home care, or long-term stays once skilled care is no longer needed. After day 100 in a skilled nursing facility, Medicare pays nothing.

### What is the income limit to qualify for Medicaid for a nursing home?
Income limits vary by state. In most states, monthly income cannot exceed a threshold tied to the cost of nursing home care — roughly $2,800–$3,000/month in many states (the 2026 federal cap for states that use it is $2,982/month). In some states, income above the limit can be directed to a "Miller Trust" or income-only trust to qualify. Contact your state Medicaid office or an elder law attorney for current rules in your state.

### How does long-term care insurance work?
Once you purchase a policy, you pay premiums (typically monthly or annually). When you need care, you must meet a benefit trigger — usually needing help with 2 of 6 daily activities or having severe cognitive impairment — and satisfy an elimination period (typically 30–90 days of out-of-pocket care). After that, the policy pays a daily or monthly benefit toward your care costs, up to the policy's maximum amount and benefit period.

### What senior care does VA Aid & Attendance cover?
VA Aid & Attendance is a tax-free monthly cash benefit paid to eligible wartime veterans and their surviving spouses who need help with daily activities. In 2026, it pays up to $2,424/month for a single veteran and $2,874/month for a married veteran. The money can be applied to in-home care, assisted living, or nursing home costs — wherever the veteran receives care.

### What happens when seniors run out of money to pay for a nursing home?
Once countable assets fall below the state's Medicaid threshold (often around $2,000 for an individual), the person typically qualifies for Medicaid, which then pays the nursing home directly. Medicaid-certified nursing homes cannot discharge a resident solely because they transitioned from private pay to Medicaid — federal law protects this right. Plan ahead by choosing a Medicaid-certified facility from the start.

### Can a reverse mortgage pay for senior care?
A HECM (Home Equity Conversion Mortgage) lets homeowners 62 and older borrow against home equity as a lump sum, monthly payment, or line of credit — with no monthly repayment required while living in the home. This can fund in-home care, supplement assisted living costs, or extend the private-pay period before Medicaid. However, if the senior moves permanently to a nursing home, the loan becomes due. HUD-approved counseling is required before taking out a HECM.
