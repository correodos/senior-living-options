> **Key Takeaways**
> - Long-term care costs remain historically high. Assisted living reached a national median of $6,200/month ($74,400/year) in 2025, and a private nursing home room runs $10,798/month ($129,575/year).
> - Medicare does **not** cover long-term care — only short-term skilled nursing after a qualifying 3-day hospital stay. In 2026, the Medicare copay is $217/day for days 21–100.
> - Medicaid is the primary payer for nursing home care nationwide once assets are spent down. Rules, income limits, and eligibility vary by state.
> - Memory care is the fastest-growing segment in senior housing — CCRC memory care occupancy hit 89.9% in Q3 2024, signaling tight supply and growing waitlists.
> - Finding the right option often takes months. The National Institute on Aging recommends planning before a crisis forces a rushed decision.

---

Watching someone you love struggle at home — and knowing something has to change — is one of the hardest places a family can be. You're not just picking a place to live. You're making a decision that touches on safety, dignity, finances, and relationships all at once.

The good news is that "senior living" isn't a single thing. There are seven meaningfully different types of senior housing options, ranging from professional help at home to full skilled nursing care. Each one was built for a different level of need, budget, and lifestyle. Once you understand how they compare, the picture gets clearer — even if the decision still isn't easy.

This article walks through all seven senior living options with 2025 costs from authoritative sources, who each type is actually designed for, and what Medicare and Medicaid will (and won't) pay for each.

## The 7 Types of Senior Living at a Glance

Before going deep on each option, here's how they compare on the dimensions that matter most.

| Type | Care Level | Median Monthly Cost (2025) | Medicare Covers? | Medicaid Covers? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| In-Home Care | Low–Moderate | $35/hr ($6,160/mo at 44 hrs/wk) | Skilled care only, short-term | Sometimes (HCBS waivers) |
| Independent Living | Minimal | ~$3,200–$3,747/mo | No | No |
| Board & Care / Residential Care | Low–Moderate | Typically less than assisted living | No | Some states |

## 1. In-Home Care: Staying Home With Support

In-home care means professional caregivers come to your loved one's home — either on a scheduled basis or daily — to help with tasks they can no longer manage alone. It's the option most seniors prefer: the National Institute on Aging (NIA) notes that the vast majority of older adults want to remain in their own homes as long as possible.

The 2025 CareScout survey made a notable methodological change: homemaker services and home health aide services are now reported together as **"non-medical caregiver"** services, because two-thirds of home care agencies now charge the same rate for both types. This reflects a real shift in how the industry prices its workforce.

The national median hourly rate for non-medical caregiver services is **$35/hour** (2025), a 3% increase year-over-year. At 44 hours per week — near-daily support — that totals approximately $80,080/year or about $6,673/month. Families needing only a few hours per day will pay considerably less.

The 2025 survey also introduced a separate category: **skilled nursing in the home (private duty nurse)**. The national median is $90/hour or $160 per visit. This covers medically complex home care ordered by a physician — IV therapy, wound management, post-surgical monitoring.

**Who it's right for:** Someone who is medically stable, safe at home with some support, and has either family nearby or the budget to cover consistent aide hours. It works best for mild-to-moderate care needs. When needs escalate to 24-hour supervision, in-home care often becomes more expensive than a facility.

**What Medicare covers:** Medicare Part A covers *skilled* home health care — nursing visits, physical therapy, wound care — when ordered by a physician after a qualifying clinical event. It does not cover non-medical custodial care (bathing, dressing, cooking) on its own.

**What Medicaid covers:** Many states offer Home and Community Based Services (HCBS) Medicaid waivers that pay for in-home aide services for eligible low-income seniors. Eligibility and covered services vary by state. Use the free Eldercare Locator at eldercare.acl.gov or call 1-800-677-1116 to find your state's programs.

## 2. Independent Living: Community Without Care

Independent living communities — also called retirement communities or 55+ communities — are designed for seniors who don't need help with daily activities. Think apartment or cottage-style living with shared amenities: dining rooms, fitness centers, transportation, and social programming. The purpose is lifestyle and community, not medical care.

**What independent living does not include:** personal care assistance, medication management, or nursing services. Residents manage their own health. If your loved one needs help with bathing, dressing, or managing chronic conditions, this is not the right fit yet.

**Who it's right for:** Active seniors who want to downsize, reduce home maintenance, and build community — without giving up independence. It also makes sense as an early move to a CCRC campus before care needs arise.

**Costs:** National data on standalone independent living communities is not collected by CareScout or the government surveys listed above. The best available industry benchmark from the National Investment Center for Seniors Housing & Care (NIC) puts average monthly rent for independent living within CCRCs at approximately **$3,747** at the end of 2024 — the most comprehensive market-level data available. Standalone communities outside CCRC campuses typically fall in the $2,500–$4,500/month range depending on location, unit size, and amenities, with wide variation by region.

**What pays:** Independent living is almost entirely private pay. Medicare and Medicaid do not cover room and board in independent living communities.

## 3. Board & Care Homes: Small-Scale, Home-Like Setting

Board and care homes — also called residential care homes, adult family homes, or adult foster care homes — are licensed private residences that house a small number of seniors, typically six or fewer. A live-in or on-site operator provides meals, housekeeping, personal care, and supervision.

They sit between in-home care and assisted living: more personal attention than a large facility, but more oversight than living alone. Many families find them particularly valuable for loved ones who feel lost or overwhelmed in larger, institutional settings.

**Who it's right for:** Seniors who need moderate personal care assistance but benefit from a quieter, intimate environment. Also a practical option when standard assisted living is unavailable locally or cost-prohibitive.

**Costs:** No federal agency or authorized sector organization currently publishes a verified national median cost for board and care homes. The federal ACL and CareScout surveys do not include this care type as a separate category. What the available data consistently shows is that board and care costs are generally **lower than assisted living** — which had a 2025 national median of $6,200/month — and often significantly so, particularly for smaller, non-urban homes. Regional variation is substantial.

**What pays:** Primarily private pay. Medicaid coverage varies by state — some states have waiver programs that cover residential care in board and care settings. Contact your State Medicaid office or use eldercare.acl.gov to check your state's specific programs.

## 4. Assisted Living: The Most Common Senior Living Option

[Assisted living](/article/assisted-living/assisted-living-complete-guide/) is the type most families encounter first when a parent or spouse can no longer live safely alone. These are licensed residential communities where staff help residents with activities of daily living (ADLs): bathing, dressing, grooming, medication management, and meals. Residents typically have their own apartments or rooms.

The national median monthly cost for assisted living is **$6,200/month ($74,400/year)** as of 2025. This is confirmed by both the CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey and AHCA/NCAL's Assisted Living Facts & Figures — two of the most authoritative sources in the sector. Growth slowed to 5% in 2025 after a 10% surge in 2024.

**What it provides:**
- 24-hour staff availability (not 24-hour nursing)
- Help with bathing, dressing, grooming, and personal care
- Medication management
- Meals, housekeeping, and laundry
- Social activities and scheduled transportation

**What it does not provide:** Continuous skilled nursing care or management of complex medical conditions. Residents with advanced dementia, significant fall risk, or high medical complexity often need to move to a higher level of care over time.

**Who it's right for:** Someone who needs daily assistance with personal care but does not require round-the-clock nursing. It's the most appropriate fit for many seniors in their 70s and 80s with moderate health conditions. More than 1 million Americans currently reside in assisted living communities.

**What pays:** Assisted living is primarily private pay. The majority of residents use long-term care insurance, personal finances, or a combination. Medicare does not cover room and board in assisted living. Some states have Medicaid waiver programs that pay for care services in an assisted living setting — but not the room — and waitlists are common.

## 5. Memory Care: Specialized Support for Dementia

[Memory care](/article/memory-care/memory-care-complete-guide/) is a specialized form of residential care designed specifically for people with Alzheimer's disease and other forms of dementia. It can operate as a standalone facility or as a dedicated, secured wing within an assisted living community.

The differences from general assisted living are clinically meaningful:

- **Secured environment** to prevent wandering — a major safety risk as dementia progresses
- **Staff trained specifically in dementia care** and behavioral management
- **Structured daily programming** designed to reduce confusion, anxiety, and agitation
- **Higher staff-to-resident ratios** than standard assisted living

Memory care demand continues to outpace supply. As of Q3 2024, memory care occupancy within CCRCs reached 89.9% — the 10th consecutive quarter of growth — and standalone memory care communities were at 85.1% nationally. When occupancy approaches 90%, waitlists become common.

**Who it's right for:** Someone with a diagnosed dementia condition — Alzheimer's, Lewy body, frontotemporal, or vascular dementia — who is no longer safe at home or in a general assisted living setting due to wandering, significant behavioral symptoms, or severe cognitive impairment. The Alzheimer's Association estimates that more than 7.2 million Americans age 65 and older are living with Alzheimer's in 2025.

**Costs:** Industry-wide data for 2025 consistently shows memory care running between **$6,450 and $6,690/month** nationally — approximately 20–30% more than general assisted living, reflecting the higher staffing and security requirements. Note that this range is based on sector data (NIC, industry surveys) rather than a government-published figure, since CareScout does not separately report a national median for standalone memory care. Costs vary significantly by state and facility type.

**What pays:** Primarily private pay — same rules as assisted living. Medicaid waiver programs in some states cover memory care in licensed facilities, but availability is limited. Medicare does not cover the residential care component. Long-term care insurance typically covers memory care if the policy was purchased before diagnosis.

## 6. Nursing Homes (Skilled Nursing Facilities): The Highest Level of Residential Care

[Nursing homes](/article/nursing-homes/nursing-homes-complete-guide/) — formally called skilled nursing facilities (SNFs) when Medicare-certified — provide the most intensive level of residential care outside of a hospital. They have licensed nurses on staff 24 hours a day and can manage medically complex needs: wound care, IV therapy, post-surgical recovery, ventilator support, and rehabilitation services.

Cost growth moderated in 2025 after several years of sharp increases. The national median daily rate for a semi-private room is **$315/day ($114,975/year)**, and the median for a private room is **$355/day ($129,575/year)** — roughly $9,581/month and $10,798/month respectively. These represent 2% and 1% increases over 2024.

The AHCA/NCAL notes that more than 1.2 million Americans reside in nursing homes at any given time.

**Who it's right for:** Someone with complex, ongoing medical needs — recovering from a stroke, hip fracture surgery, or managing an advanced chronic condition — or someone whose dementia or physical decline requires 24-hour skilled nursing supervision that assisted living cannot safely provide.

**What Medicare covers:** Medicare Part A covers short-term stays in a Medicare-certified SNF under specific conditions. You must have had a qualifying inpatient hospital stay of at least three consecutive days (not counting the discharge day), and you must enter the SNF within 30 days of leaving the hospital for the same condition. A physician must certify the need for daily skilled care. 

During a covered SNF stay in 2026:
- **Days 1–20:** Medicare pays all costs (after the Part A deductible of $1,736)
- **Days 21–100:** You pay **$217/day**; Medicare covers the rest
- **Day 101+:** You pay all costs


Medicare does not cover long-term custodial nursing home care.

**What Medicaid covers:** Medicaid is the primary payer for long-term nursing home care in the United States. It covers nursing home stays for eligible low-income individuals once they meet income and asset requirements. If your loved one's assets exceed the eligibility threshold, they may need to "spend down" savings on care costs before qualifying. Asset limits, look-back periods, and rules vary by state — consult an elder law attorney before making any large asset transfers.

## 7. CCRCs / Life Plan Communities: All Levels on One Campus

A continuing care retirement community (CCRC) — increasingly marketed as a "life plan community" — offers the full spectrum of senior living on a single campus: independent living, assisted living, memory care, and skilled nursing. The core appeal is that a resident can move in healthy and transition to higher levels of care without ever relocating.

The National Investment Center for Seniors Housing & Care (NIC) reports that the average monthly rent for independent living within a CCRC was approximately **$3,747 at the end of 2024**, and some reporting points to a 2024 average of **$4,200/month** across all CCRC entry points. Entry fees for entrance-fee CCRCs average approximately **$400,000**, with a range from under $100,000 to over $1 million depending on location, unit size, and contract type.

CCRCs use one of three contract structures:

- **Type A (Life Care):** Highest upfront entry fee; future care costs are largely covered or significantly discounted regardless of care level needed. The most financial predictability.
- **Type B (Modified):** Entry fee plus monthly fee; some care levels are included, others cost more as needs escalate.
- **Type C (Fee-for-Service):** Lower entry fee; residents pay market rate for each level of care as they need it. Lowest commitment upfront, highest potential cost if significant care is needed.

**Who it's right for:** Families who want a single long-term plan and are willing — and financially able — to commit early. CCRCs are best evaluated years before a care crisis, not as emergency placements. They are not accessible to most middle-income families without significant home equity or savings.

**What pays:** Mostly private pay. The SNF component may be covered by Medicare under the same short-term rules that apply to a standalone nursing home. Medicaid coverage at CCRCs is uncommon but can apply to the nursing care component in some states. The tax deductibility of a portion of entrance fees (as prepaid medical expenses) can partially offset costs — consult a tax advisor.

## How to Choose the Right Senior Living Option

The right choice depends on three overlapping factors: **current care needs**, **anticipated future needs**, and **budget**.

Start with an honest assessment of what your loved one can and cannot do independently. The NIA recommends evaluating the ability to manage medications, prepare meals, bathe safely, and move around without falling.

Then consider:

- **Is the current home safe?** Falls, missed medications, and social isolation are warning signs that the current arrangement isn't working.
- **What does care cost now versus in 2 years?** In-home care at 40+ hours per week can exceed assisted living costs quickly.
- **What does existing coverage pay?** Long-term care insurance, veterans benefits (VA.gov), and Medicaid all have specific rules, timelines, and eligibility requirements.
- **How quickly could needs change?** If a dementia diagnosis is recent, memory care availability matters now — not after a crisis.

The **Eldercare Locator**, run by the Administration for Community Living (ACL), connects families with local resources, geriatric care managers, and state-specific benefit programs at no cost: **eldercare.acl.gov** or 1-800-677-1116.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the difference between assisted living and a nursing home?
Assisted living provides help with daily personal care — bathing, dressing, medications — but does not have licensed nurses on duty 24 hours a day. Nursing homes (skilled nursing facilities) have nurses on site around the clock and can manage complex medical needs including wound care, IV therapy, and post-surgical recovery. Assisted living is appropriate for moderate care needs; nursing homes are for people requiring skilled nursing or high medical complexity.

### Does Medicare pay for assisted living?
No. Medicare does not cover room and board or ongoing personal care in assisted living. Medicare may cover short-term skilled services — physical therapy or wound care ordered by a physician — but it does not pay for the assisted living facility itself. For long-term coverage, look into your state's Medicaid waiver programs or long-term care insurance.

### How much does a nursing home cost per month in 2025?
The national median reached $9,581/month for a semi-private room and $10,798/month for a private room in 2025, representing 2% and 1% increases over 2024. Costs vary significantly by state.

### What is the cheapest type of senior care?
Adult day health care is the lowest-cost option for people who still live at home. The national median daily rate declined 5% in 2025 to $95/day — roughly $24,700/year based on 5 days per week. It provides supervision, structured programming, and social connection while the person sleeps at home. For full residential care, board and care homes are typically more affordable than assisted living, though no national median figure is published by federal sources.

### Can Medicaid pay for assisted living?
Medicaid does not directly fund assisted living in most states, but many states operate Medicaid Home and Community Based Services (HCBS) waiver programs that pay for care services within a licensed assisted living setting. Availability is limited and waitlists can be long. Eligibility is income- and asset-based and rules vary by state. Contact your State Medicaid office or visit Medicaid.gov to check your state's options.

### What is memory care and when is it needed?
Memory care is a specialized residential setting designed for people with Alzheimer's disease or other dementias. It provides secured environments to prevent wandering, dementia-trained staff, and structured daily programming. It becomes the right choice when someone with dementia can no longer be kept safe at home or in standard assisted living — particularly when wandering, significant behavioral symptoms, or an inability to recognize familiar people are present. More than 7.2 million Americans age 65 and older are living with Alzheimer's in 2025.
