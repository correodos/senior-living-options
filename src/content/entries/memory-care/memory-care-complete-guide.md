> **Key Takeaways**
> - Memory care is a specialized form of residential care for people with Alzheimer's disease or other dementias. It provides a secured environment, dementia-trained staff, and structured programming that standard assisted living does not.
> - An estimated 7.4 million Americans age 65 and older are living with Alzheimer's disease in 2026, and that number is projected to nearly double by 2060.
> - Memory care typically costs 15–25% more than standard assisted living. National estimates for 2025–2026 range from $6,690 to $8,019/month depending on the data source and region. CareScout/Genworth does not publish a separate memory care median. The $6,200/month assisted living figure is the closest official benchmark.
> - Neither Medicare nor most private health insurance covers room and board or personal care in memory care. Medicaid may help through HCBS waiver programs, but availability varies by state and waitlists are common.
> - When choosing a facility, staff turnover rate and dementia-specific training hours matter more than lobby aesthetics. Visit more than once, including unannounced.

---

Watching someone you love lose pieces of themselves to Alzheimer's or dementia is one of the most painful things a family can go through. At some point — sometimes gradually, sometimes after a single frightening night — caring for them at home stops being safe. And dementia doesn't behave like other aging challenges: it changes behavior, erases safety awareness, and creates risks that ordinary senior care settings aren't built to handle.

Memory care exists for exactly that situation. This guide covers what it is, how it differs from assisted living, what it costs, who pays, and what to look for when you visit facilities. Everything here comes from federal health agencies and independent research.

---

## What is memory care?

Memory care combines the personal care services of [assisted living](/article/assisted-living/assisted-living-complete-guide/) — help with bathing, dressing, meals, and medication — with features that standard assisted living doesn't provide: secured exits, staff trained in dementia behavior management, structured daily routines, and programming built around cognitive impairment.

Facilities take different forms. Some are standalone communities dedicated entirely to dementia care. Others are secured wings within a larger assisted living building or nursing home. Still others are part of a Continuing Care Retirement Community (CCRC), which lets a resident stay on the same campus as their needs increase.

Alzheimer's disease is the most common reason families seek memory care; it accounts for 60–80% of all dementia cases. But memory care also serves people with other forms of dementia, including vascular dementia, Lewy body dementia, and frontotemporal dementia. Each has different behavioral patterns, and a good memory care facility should have staff trained to recognize those differences.

An estimated 7.4 million Americans age 65 and older are living with Alzheimer's in 2026. By 2060, that number is projected to reach nearly 13.8 million if no cure or effective prevention is found. Three in four people with Alzheimer's are age 75 or older. → [https://www.alz.org/alzheimers-dementia/facts-figures](https://www.alz.org/alzheimers-dementia/facts-figures)

## Memory care vs. assisted living: key differences

Both memory care and standard assisted living provide housing, meals, personal care assistance, and activities. Memory care differs in the ways below.

| | Assisted living | Memory care |
|---|---|---|
| Physical environment | Open floor plan, standard apartment setup | Secured doors, simplified layouts, visual wayfinding cues |
| Staff training | General senior care | Dementia-specific training, behavior management, de-escalation |
| Staff-to-resident ratio | Lower (varies by state) | Higher — more direct supervision required |
| Programming | General social activities | Structured routines designed for cognitive impairment |
| Cost (2025–2026 estimates) | $6,200/month (CareScout 2025) | $6,690–$8,019/month (sector estimates) |
| Wandering prevention | Basic emergency call systems | Secured exits, wander alert systems, enclosed outdoor areas |

### Why the physical design matters

The layout of a memory care unit is part of the care. Simplified, circular floor plans reduce disorientation. Visual cues like picture labels on doors or color-coded hallways help residents with wayfinding without requiring them to read or remember. Enclosed outdoor spaces allow residents to move around safely without risk of elopement (leaving the facility undetected).

Wandering is one of the most serious safety risks in dementia care. According to the Alzheimer's Association, 6 in 10 people with dementia will wander at some point. Standard assisted living facilities aren't designed to prevent this, and memory care units are. → [https://www.alz.org](https://www.alz.org)

### Staff training: the most important difference

The quality of dementia-specific training varies considerably across states. Research published in 2023 found that 45 states have some form of dementia-specific regulations for memory care settings, but requirements differ widely in the number of training hours required, the topics covered, and whether training applies to all staff or only direct care workers.

When you visit a facility, ask directly: How many hours of dementia-specific training do staff receive at hire? How many hours of ongoing training per year? What does that training cover? Facilities that can't answer these questions specifically are a red flag.

## Signs it's time to consider memory care

The transition from home care or assisted living to memory care usually builds over weeks or months. According to the National Institute on Aging, these are the clearest signs that someone with dementia needs a higher level of care:

### Safety concerns that can't be managed at home:
- Wandering outside the home, especially at night or in unsafe conditions
- Leaving the stove on, forgetting to eat, or drinking unsafe liquids
- Falls related to confusion or disorientation
- Getting lost in familiar places like the neighborhood or grocery store

### Behavioral changes that exceed caregiver capacity:
- Aggression, agitation, or paranoia, which are common in moderate-stage Alzheimer's
- Hallucinations or delusions
- Severe sleep disruption affecting everyone in the household
- Resistance to personal care that results in hygiene neglect

### Functional decline:
- Loss of ability to dress, bathe, or use the bathroom independently
- Significant unintentional weight loss from forgetting to eat
- Inability to manage medications safely even with reminders

### Caregiver crisis:
Nearly 13 million Americans provide unpaid care for people with Alzheimer's or other dementias. In 2025, those caregivers provided more than 19 billion hours of care, valued at over $446 billion. When your own health, sleep, work, or safety is deteriorating because of caregiving demands, that's a signal too — for everyone involved, not just your loved one.

If you're unsure, a geriatric care manager or your loved one's neurologist can help assess whether the current situation is still safe. The Alzheimer's Association 24/7 Helpline (800-272-3900) is also a free resource for families at any stage. → [https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/helpline](https://www.alz.org/help-support/caregiving/helpline)

## What does memory care cost?

Memory care costs more than standard assisted living — primarily because of higher staffing ratios, dementia training requirements, and secured building design. Industry estimates put the premium at 15–25% above standard assisted living rates.

CareScout/Genworth, the most authoritative source for [long-term care cost data](/article/senior-care-costs/senior-care-costs-complete-guide/), does not publish a separate national median for memory care in its annual Cost of Care Survey. Their 2025 survey tracks five categories: non-medical in-home care, private duty nursing, adult day care, assisted living, and nursing homes. Memory care falls between assisted living and nursing home care in both intensity and price, but isn't tracked as a separate line item.

The table below puts the CareScout 2025 official benchmarks alongside memory care estimates from researchers who survey those facilities directly:

| Care Type | 2025–2026 National Median (Monthly) | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Assisted Living | $6,200 | CareScout/Genworth 2025 (official) |
| Memory Care | $6,690–$8,019 | A Place for Mom (2026); SeniorLiving.org (2026); U.S. News (2026) |
| Nursing Home (semi-private) | $9,581 | CareScout/Genworth 2025 (official) |
| Nursing Home (private room) | $10,798 | CareScout/Genworth 2025 (official) |

The spread reflects real variation in who gets surveyed. Stand-alone memory care facilities tend to run higher than memory care wings within a larger assisted living campus. U.S. News puts the national average at $7,645/month (2026); A Place for Mom's network average is $6,690/month; SeniorLiving.org puts the 2026 median at $8,019/month. Your local market will land somewhere in that range — or outside it entirely.

### What affects the price

Several factors push memory care costs higher:

- Location. Using assisted living state medians from CareScout 2025 as a floor, and applying the 15–25% memory care premium: Hawaii ($12,096/month AL median, likely $14,000+/month for memory care), Connecticut ($9,118), and Massachusetts ($9,600) are among the most expensive. Mississippi ($4,369 AL median) and Alabama ($4,425) remain the most affordable, with memory care potentially starting around $5,000–$5,500/month in those states. SeniorLiving.org research (2026) confirms state memory care medians ranging from $5,538/month (South Dakota) to $14,399/month (Hawaii).
- Facility type. Standalone memory care communities often charge differently from memory care wings within a larger assisted living campus.
- Care level tiers. Most facilities assess residents and assign a care level tier; higher needs mean a higher monthly rate. Ask exactly how tiers are determined and what they cost.
- Room type. Private rooms cost more than shared rooms.
- One-time fees. Community fees or move-in fees can range from $1,000 to $5,000+.

Always request the complete fee schedule in writing before visiting. Ask specifically what's included in the base rate and what triggers additional charges.

State cost data for assisted living (2025, monthly median) from CareScout can serve as a baseline reference before adding the memory care premium: → [/category/senior-care-costs/](/category/senior-care-costs/)

## Who pays for memory care?

### Medicare: what it does and doesn't cover

Medicare does not cover room and board in memory care, and it doesn't pay for the personal care assistance that makes up most of what these facilities provide — the same rule that applies to assisted living.

What Medicare does cover are the medical services a resident receives while living there: doctor visits, prescription drugs (including FDA-approved Alzheimer's medications under Part D), hospital stays, and short-term skilled nursing following a qualifying hospital admission. Those are billed separately and don't offset the facility's monthly fees.

CMS's GUIDE (Guiding an Improved Dementia Experience) model launched on July 1, 2024, and is currently active. Running through 2032, with approximately 390 participating provider organizations nationwide, GUIDE pays enrolled providers to deliver a standardized package of dementia care coordination, caregiver education and support, and a respite care allowance, all at no cost to eligible Medicare beneficiaries with a dementia diagnosis. It does not cover facility room and board, but the respite funds and care navigation can meaningfully reduce caregiver burden and help delay or reduce reliance on full-time memory care. Ask your loved one's physician or neurologist whether they participate in GUIDE, or find participating organizations through the Alzheimer's Association (alz.org/guide).

### Medicaid: the primary public option

For families who can't afford memory care out of pocket, Medicaid is often the only meaningful public funding source. It covers memory care in two ways.

Nursing home coverage is available in every state for Medicaid-eligible individuals. If your loved one requires nursing home-level care, which many people with advanced dementia do, Medicaid covers the cost of a semi-private room and associated personal care.

HCBS (Home and Community-Based Services) waivers allow states to use Medicaid funds for care delivered in community settings, including memory care units in assisted living facilities. As of 2026, most states offer some form of HCBS waiver that can be applied to memory care services, but not to room and board. The resident's income (typically Social Security) generally covers housing costs.

2026 financial eligibility basics for most HCBS waivers:
- Income limit: up to $2,982/month (300% of the Federal Benefit Rate of $994)
- Asset limit: $2,000 in countable assets for an individual
- Functional requirement: must require nursing facility-level care

Waitlists are real and often long. If you think your loved one may eventually qualify for Medicaid, apply as early as possible. Contact your state Medicaid office or the Eldercare Locator (1-800-677-1116 / eldercare.acl.gov) for state-specific guidance.

### VA benefits for veterans

Wartime veterans and surviving spouses may qualify for the VA Aid and Attendance pension benefit, which provides tax-free monthly payments that can be applied directly toward memory care costs.

2026 maximum monthly rates (effective December 1, 2025):

| Status | Max Annual (MAPR) | Max Monthly |
|---|---|---|
| Veteran, no dependents | $29,093 | $2,424 |
| Veteran, one dependent | $34,488 | $2,874 |
| Surviving spouse, no dependents | $18,697 | $1,558 |

These are the maximum amounts. Your actual monthly benefit equals the MAPR minus your countable income. Net worth limit for 2026: $163,699 (excluding primary home and vehicle). A 3-year look-back period applies to asset transfers.

Apply via VA Form 21P-527EZ at VA.gov, or work with a VA-accredited claims agent. There is no cost to use an accredited claims agent.

### Long-term care insurance

If your loved one purchased a long-term care insurance policy, review it carefully, since many policies cover memory care. Key things to check: what triggers the benefit (typically inability to perform 2+ ADLs, or cognitive impairment), the elimination period (waiting period before benefits start, often 60–90 days), and the daily benefit maximum and inflation protection. Contact the insurer directly to initiate a claim.

## What services does memory care include?

Memory care covers the same basics as assisted living — meals, personal care, medication management, housekeeping — but layered over a structure that's built specifically for dementia. Predictable daily routines reduce anxiety. Programming uses music, photos, and tactile activities to reach people who can no longer follow a conversation. Staff ratios are higher because residents need more direct supervision throughout the day.

Most licensed memory care settings provide:
- 24-hour awake staff and controlled entry/exit
- Three meals daily, adapted for residents who need soft or pureed options
- Help with all ADLs: bathing, dressing, grooming, toileting, mobility
- Medication administration and management
- Housekeeping and laundry
- Health monitoring and coordination with outside physicians

Dementia-specific programming typically includes structured daily routines, reminiscence therapy (photos, music, meaningful objects from the person's past), music therapy, sensory and tactile activities, pet therapy, and gentle movement programs.

Safety features to ask about specifically: secured doors with keypads or delayed-egress technology, wander alert systems, enclosed outdoor spaces, and color-coded or picture-labeled hallways for wayfinding.

Not every facility offers all of these. Ask which programs run, how often, and what percentage of residents actually participate — the answer tells you more than the activities calendar on the wall.

## How to choose a memory care facility

### Step 1: Define your loved one's needs now and in 18 months

Dementia is progressive, and the facility that fits today may not fit next year. Ask the neurologist or geriatrician where they estimate your loved one is in the disease progression and what changes are likely in the near term. Prioritize facilities that can accommodate increasing care needs without requiring another move.

If your loved one has a specific form of dementia (Lewy body dementia, frontotemporal dementia, or younger-onset Alzheimer's), ask whether staff have specific experience with those diagnoses, since behaviors and needs differ meaningfully from typical Alzheimer's.

### Step 2: Find licensed facilities in your area

Use the Eldercare Locator (eldercare.acl.gov or 1-800-677-1116) to find licensed facilities in your area. Your local Area Agency on Aging can provide referrals and information about inspection histories and complaints. Your loved one's neurologist or hospital social worker may also have direct experience with local facilities.

### Step 3: Visit in person at least twice

The NIA specifically advises making at least one unannounced visit at a different day and time than your scheduled tour. AARP echoes this: pay attention to how you feel walking through the space, and whether it feels like a place you'd want a family member to live.

What to observe during visits:

- Staff interactions with residents. Are staff warm and unhurried? Do they call residents by name? Do they use calm, patient language?
- Resident demeanor. Are people engaged and calm, or sedated and isolated in front of a television?
- Common areas. Is the environment cheerful and navigable? Is there noise management (excessive noise increases agitation in dementia)?
- Meals. Observe a mealtime. Is food adapted for residents who may need soft or pureed options? Is the dining room calm?
- Security features. How are exits controlled? Are there enclosed outdoor areas?
- Staffing. Are there enough staff on the floor to respond when multiple residents need help simultaneously?

### Step 4: Ask the hard questions directly

Don't rely on the brochure. Ask to speak with the Director of Nursing and the Memory Care Unit director, not just the admissions coordinator.

Key questions to ask:

- What is the staff-to-resident ratio during day shift? Evening shift? Overnight?
- What is the staff turnover rate for direct care workers in the memory care unit? (High turnover is one of the clearest quality warning signs in dementia care.)
- How many hours of dementia-specific training do new staff receive? How much ongoing training per year?
- What is your protocol for managing behavioral symptoms (wandering, agitation, refusal of care)? Do you use antipsychotic medications? Under what circumstances?
- How are care plans developed? How often are they reviewed? Are family members included?
- What happens if my loved one's needs exceed what this community can provide?
- What are the discharge policies? Under what conditions would a resident be asked to leave?
- What end-of-life and hospice care options are available on site?

### Step 5: Review the contract before signing anything

Read the entire contract. Have a family member or attorney review it, particularly the fee structure, care level assessment process, discharge policies, and any arbitration clauses. AARP advises being alert to mandatory arbitration clauses, which can limit your legal recourse if something goes wrong.

Confirm in writing: what is included in the base rate, what triggers additional charges, how and how often rates can increase, and what financial transitions are available if funds run low.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is memory care and how is it different from assisted living?

Memory care is a specialized form of residential care for people with Alzheimer's disease or other dementias. Unlike standard assisted living, memory care facilities have secured environments to prevent wandering, staff with dementia-specific training, and programming designed for cognitive impairment. Memory care typically costs 15–25% more than standard assisted living.

### How much does memory care cost per month?

National estimates for 2025–2026 range from $6,690 to $8,019/month depending on the source. U.S. News estimates the national average at $7,645/month; A Place for Mom's network average is $6,690/month; SeniorLiving.org puts the 2026 median at $8,019/month. Memory care typically runs 15–25% above standard assisted living (national median: $6,200/month per CareScout/Genworth 2025). CareScout does not publish a separate memory care median; these estimates come from sector researchers who survey memory care providers directly. Costs vary widely by state, with Hawaii and the Northeast running well above $10,000/month. Always request a full fee schedule in writing before choosing a facility.

### Does Medicare pay for memory care?

No. Medicare does not cover room and board or personal care assistance in a memory care facility. Medicare does cover certain medical services a resident may receive, such as doctor visits, prescription drugs (including Alzheimer's medications under Part D), and short-term skilled nursing following a qualifying hospital stay. Those are separate from the facility's monthly fees.

### Will Medicaid pay for memory care?

Possibly. Medicaid covers nursing home care in every state for eligible individuals. For memory care in assisted living settings, most states offer coverage through HCBS (Home and Community-Based Services) waivers, but this covers care services only, not room and board. For 2026, income limits for most HCBS waivers are $2,982/month with a $2,000 asset limit for individuals. Waitlists are common. Contact your state Medicaid office or call the Eldercare Locator at 1-800-677-1116 for state-specific information.

### When should someone with dementia move to memory care?

There's no universal trigger, but clear signals include: wandering that creates safety risks, aggressive or agitated behavior that family caregivers can no longer manage safely, severe sleep disruption, significant unintentional weight loss, inability to perform ADLs, and caregiver burnout that compromises everyone's health. Your loved one's neurologist can help assess whether the current situation is still safe. The Alzheimer's Association Helpline (800-272-3900) is available 24/7 for guidance.

### What questions should I ask when visiting a memory care facility?

The most important questions cover staff turnover (high turnover is a quality red flag), dementia-specific training hours, the medication management philosophy (particularly regarding antipsychotics), how care plans are developed and updated, what triggers discharge, and what the policy is on end-of-life care. Also request inspection reports and ask about any recent citations. Visiting unannounced at a different time of day than your scheduled tour is strongly recommended.
