If someone you love has Alzheimer's or another type of dementia, you have probably heard the words "memory care" from a doctor, a social worker or a friend who has been through it. It can sound like one more decision on a list that already feels too long.

So what is memory care, and when does it make sense? This article answers that in practical terms: how memory care matches the stages of Alzheimer's, why wandering changes the safety picture, how a memory care unit in assisted living differs from one in a nursing home, and what to expect when your loved one moves in.

## What Is Memory Care?

Memory care is long-term care designed for people living with Alzheimer's disease or other dementias. The National Institute on Aging (NIA) describes memory care units in two kinds of places:

- **Special Alzheimer's units in assisted living**, where staff check on and care for people with dementia.
- **Special Alzheimer's care units in nursing homes**, often in a separate section of the building, where staff have special training to care for people with dementia.

What sets these units apart is who they serve: they focus on people with dementia, rather than on older adults in general.

Alzheimer's, the most common cause of dementia, is widespread and growing. An estimated 7.4 million Americans age 65 and older are living with Alzheimer's in 2026, and that number could reach 13.8 million by 2060 without a medical breakthrough.

---

## How Memory Care Fits the Stages of Alzheimer's

Alzheimer's changes over time, and so does the help a person needs. The NIA describes three stages after symptoms begin:

- **Mild stage.** Greater memory loss, wandering and getting lost, trouble handling money, repeating questions, taking longer with daily tasks and personality changes. People are often diagnosed in this stage.

  *Questions families often face:* Is home still safe? What support can we add at home?

- **Moderate stage.** Memory loss and confusion grow worse. The person may have trouble recognizing family and friends, trouble with multistep tasks like getting dressed, and possible hallucinations, paranoia and impulsive behavior.

  *Questions families often face:* Can we provide supervision day and night? Is a secured setting with trained staff needed?

- **Severe stage.** The person cannot communicate and is completely dependent on others for care. Near the end of life, they may be in bed most or all of the time.

  *Questions families often face:* Is nursing home care or hospice the right fit?

*Note: The questions are a guide for families, not a medical rule. Every person's disease moves at its own pace. Talk with their doctor about what level of care fits now.*

In the moderate stage, the NIA describes changes such as worsening confusion, needing help to get dressed, not recognizing familiar people and impulsive behavior. These are the kinds of needs memory care units are set up for, with supervision and staff who care for people with dementia. In the severe stage, the person needs total care, and the NIA notes that nursing homes are for people who can't care for themselves anymore and may need ongoing medical care.

If your loved one is still at home, ask their doctor about Medicare's GUIDE dementia care program. It began on July 1, 2024, runs for 8 years, and offers help coordinating care, a 24/7 support line, caregiver training and respite services of up to $2,500 a year through participating providers.

## Why Wandering Changes the Safety Picture

Wandering is one of the most frightening parts of dementia for families. Six in 10 people living with dementia will wander at least once, and many do so repeatedly. It can happen at any stage of the disease.

At home, the NIA recommends steps like these:

- An ID or medical bracelet with their name, address and your phone number, or a GPS tracker
- Telling neighbors and local police that your loved one may wander
- A keyed deadbolt, or an extra lock placed up high or down low on the door
- A doorbell or alarm that chimes when a door opens
- A fenced yard with a locked gate
- Keeping shoes, keys, coats and suitcases out of sight

The NIA is also clear that a person with Alzheimer's who has a history of wandering should not be left unattended. If you can no longer make sure someone is with your loved one at all times, talk with their doctor about whether memory care is the next step.

> If your loved one wanders and goes missing, start searching right away. Many people who wander are found within 1.5 miles of where they disappeared.

## Memory Care in Assisted Living vs. in a Nursing Home

Memory care is not one single type of place. Where the unit is located changes the level of care.

| | **In assisted living** | **In a nursing home** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Who it's for** | Needs daily help, not medical care around the clock | Can't care for themselves, may need ongoing medical care |
| **Staff** | Check on and care for people with dementia | Special training in dementia care |
| **Where it is** | A special unit in the community | Often a separate section of the building |
| **What you pay** | Room or apartment, plus possible extra fees | Nursing home rate; Medicare doesn't cover long stays |

If your loved one has serious medical needs as well as dementia, a nursing home unit may fit better. If their main needs are supervision, structure and help with daily tasks, a unit in assisted living may be enough. ## What to Expect When Your Loved One Moves In

Moving someone with dementia into memory care is a big change for both of you. The NIA notes that caregivers may feel many emotions, from loss and sadness to guilt or even relief, and that moving day can be very stressful.

**Before the move**:

- Get to know the staff before your loved one moves in, and talk with them about ways to make the transition go smoothly.
- Be an advocate for your loved one's needs and preferences.
- A social worker may be able to help you plan for and adjust to the change.

**After the move**:

- Expect an adjustment period. A change in care setting can bring temporary confusion, sleep problems, wandering, falls and appetite changes.
- Ask staff how they will help your loved one with the transition.
- Be supportive and listen to their concerns, but don't argue about why they need to be there.
- If you have concerns about care that are not addressed, a long-term care ombudsman can help residents and families protect their rights.

## What Memory Care Does Not Cover

- **Medicare does not pay for long-term care.** Help with daily activities in an assisted living facility or nursing home is not covered, so families pay the full cost unless another program helps.
- **Hospice is covered, but room and board is not.** Hospice is not tied to one place. The NIA lists assisted living facilities and nursing homes among the places where hospice care can be received. Medicare covers hospice for people with a life expectancy of 6 months or less, but it does not cover room and board in a nursing home.
- **There is no official national median for memory care.** The CareScout Cost of Care Survey, the main national cost survey, reports assisted living at a median of **$6,200 per month** (2025) but does not publish a separate memory care figure. Memory care in assisted living may cost more, because you may need to pay extra for special care.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What happens if someone in memory care needs more care?

Needs often change as dementia progresses. If a community can no longer meet them, it may ask the person to move. The Alzheimer's Association suggests asking for a new assessment and a care plan meeting, and contacting the long-term care ombudsman if you need help understanding your rights in the discharge process.

### Is memory care the same as a nursing home?

Not always. Memory care can be a special unit in assisted living, for people who need help with daily tasks but not around-the-clock medical care, or a special unit in a nursing home, for people who may need ongoing medical care. Both have staff who care for people with dementia, according to the NIA.

### How long does it take someone with dementia to adjust to memory care?

There is no set timeline. The Alzheimer's Association says it takes time for a person with dementia to adjust to a new home, and temporary sleep problems, wandering, falls and appetite changes are common. Getting to know the staff before the move and asking how they will help with the transition can make it easier.

### Can hospice care be provided in memory care?

Yes. Hospice is not tied to one place, and the NIA lists assisted living facilities and nursing homes among the places where it can be received. Medicare covers hospice for people with a life expectancy of 6 months or less, but it does not cover room and board.

### How can I keep a parent with dementia from wandering at home?

The NIA recommends an ID bracelet or GPS tracker, a keyed deadbolt or an extra lock placed high or low on the door, a door alarm, a fenced yard with a locked gate, and keeping shoes, keys and coats out of sight. A person with a history of wandering should not be left unattended.
